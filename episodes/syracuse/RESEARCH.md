# RESEARCH — Archimedes vs. the Roman army (siege of Syracuse)

Episode: **"The One Guy Who Made Rome Look Stupid"** (cartoon-3d-short series).

## How this was researched (and its limits)
The primary-text sites (Perseus, LacusCurtius/penelope.uchicago.edu, Wikisource, Gutenberg, math.nyu.edu, lexundria, erenow)
are **blocked by this session's network policy**, so the ancient texts could not be read in full here. The facts below come from:
- English Wikipedia: *Siege of Syracuse (213–212 BC)*, *Claw of Archimedes*, *Archimedes* (read directly).
- Web-search excerpts quoting the standard translations: Polybius VIII (Shuckburgh / Paton), Plutarch *Marcellus* 14–18
  (Perrin, Perseus), the Suda entry quoting Polybius VIII.37.
- Prior knowledge of Livy XXIV.33–34 and XXV.23–24, used only where it agrees with the sources above.

Each script claim is listed with its support level. Anything that needs a check against the full ancient text is marked.

## Claims used in the narration

| # | Narration claim | Status | Source(s) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Rome attacked with **sixty warships** (quinqueremes) | Documented | Polybius VIII.4; Livy XXIV.34 (Marcellus with 60 quinqueremes). Wikipedia *Claw of Archimedes* cites Livy for 60. |
| 2 | **214 BC**, Sicily | Documented, with a dating caveat | Livy puts the assault in 214 BC. Wikipedia and modern chronologies (Hoyos) date the siege 213–212 BC. "214 BC" follows Livy's year for the first assault. |
| 3 | Marcellus **"figures five days should do it"** | Documented, paraphrased | Polybius VIII.3 (as excerpted): the Romans expected their siege works to give them the upper hand **within five days**. That was the army's expectation, not a direct quote from Marcellus. |
| 4 | The defences were **designed by Archimedes** | Documented (ancient tradition) | Polybius VIII.3–7; Livy XXIV.34; Plutarch *Marc.* 14–17. |
| 5 | **"seventy-something"** | Estimate | Archimedes was born c. 287 BC (the date is inferred, via Tzetzes, from an age of ~75 at death in 212). That makes him about 73 in 214 BC. |
| 6 | He'd **rather be drawing circles** | Characterisation drawn from a tradition | Plutarch says Archimedes treated his machines as diversions of geometry (Plutarch's own Platonist slant, per Wikipedia). Livy and Valerius Maximus have him drawing figures in the dust when the city fell. |
| 7 | A giant arm **hooks a warship by the nose and stands it on its tail, then lets go** | Documented | Polybius VIII.6 describes an iron hand on a chain from a crane that lifted the prow until the ship stood on its stern, then released it. Plutarch *Marc.* 15 and Livy XXIV.34 are similar. Exact design unknown (see below). |
| 8 | **Backs off → boulders; further → bigger boulders** | Documented in substance, comic in order | Polybius VIII.5: Archimedes had engines set for every range, heavier ones for long range and lighter ones for short range, so the ships were under fire at any distance. The "even bigger" escalation is a comic exaggeration of that idea. |
| 9 | **Right under the wall → tiny holes → arrows** | Documented | Polybius VIII.5: loopholes "about a palm's breadth" were cut in the wall, and archers and small "scorpions" shot through them at men close to the wall. |
| 10 | **Ladder on two ships tied together, flattened by a rock** | Documented | Polybius VIII.4 & 6 and Plutarch *Marc.* 14–15: the *sambuca* was a scaling ladder on two lashed ships. Machines dropped huge stones on it and wrecked it. |
| 11 | Marcellus **joked** that Archimedes used Roman ships **as cups to scoop up the sea** | Documented (paraphrased quotation) | Polybius VIII.6.6: Archimedes "uses my ships to ladle sea-water into his wine-cups". Plutarch *Marc.* 17 has a variant ("geometrical Briareus… uses our ships like cups to ladle water from the sea"). The narration **reports** the joke and is not a verbatim quote. |
| 12 | Men so jumpy that **a rope or a stick poking over the wall** made them run | Documented (Plutarch's anecdote) | Plutarch *Marc.* 17: "whenever they saw a bit of rope or a stick of timber projecting a little over the wall… they turned their backs and fled". Plutarch is writing ~300 years later, so it is an anecdote, not an eyewitness record. |
| 13 | Rome **stopped attacking and waited** | Documented | Polybius VIII.7; Plutarch *Marc.* 17: Marcellus gave up direct assaults and relied on a blockade. Wikipedia notes the blockade was not airtight. |
| 14 | They got in **during a festival** | Documented | Plutarch *Marc.* 18; Livy XXV.23–24: the Romans scaled the wall at night during the festival of Artemis (Diana). This was in 212 BC. |
| 15 | **Ladders sized by counting the stones** in the wall | Documented | Polybius VIII.37 (via the Suda) and Livy XXV.23: a Roman counted the courses of the ashlar blocks to work out the wall's height. Plutarch *Marc.* 18 says the tower's height was carefully estimated. The sources disagree on who counted. Polybius has an unnamed Roman, so the film gives the job to our fictional soldier. |
| 16 | "Rome beat the mathematician… by doing math" | Interpretation (punchline) | Measuring a height by counting courses is a simple calculation. Calling it "math" is the narrator's joke and not a historical claim. |

## Deliberately NOT claimed
- **Burning mirrors / heat ray.** Polybius, Livy and Plutarch do not mention them. Lucian (2nd c. AD) is the first to mention ships being set on fire, and Galen is the first to mention mirrors. The story is disputed, and modern tests are inconclusive. **Not shown and not mentioned.**
- **"Do not disturb my circles."** No ancient source has this line. It is a later paraphrase of Valerius Maximus. Archimedes' death is not shown or quoted.
- **No invented quotations.** The characters never speak. The only reported speech is Marcellus' documented joke (#11), told by the narrator.
- The **exact mechanics** of the claw and the catapults are unknown. Ancient accounts differ, and modern builds (BBC 1999, Discovery 2005) show only that such a device was possible. The film's cartoon crane is a stylised guess.

## Comic staging that is NOT history (exaggeration only)
- The characters' expressions, Marcellus' wilting helmet plume, the soldier's pincushion shield, and the helmet used as a cup to pour out the sea are visual jokes on top of documented events.
- The named soldier (the "stone counter") is fictional. Marcellus did not personally climb the ladder, and the film does not show him doing so.
- Archimedes sitting on the wall drawing while the machines work is a staging device. The sources credit him with the machines, not with operating them on the day.
- The last gag (a stick poking over the wall again) is a callback to Plutarch's anecdote (#12). It is not a claim about the night the city fell.
