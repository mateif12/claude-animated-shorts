In five thirty-nine B.C., the greatest city in the ancient world fell to Persia. And according to one famous story, its walls weren't even breached.
Babylon was built to be untouchable. Two giant walls. A moat. Gates covered in glazed blue bricks, guarded by dragons and bulls. Herodotus even claimed there was room on top of the walls to drive a four-horse chariot.
Cyrus the Great had conquered a huge empire. Babylon was his next prize.
The Babylonian Chronicle records that Persian forces defeated the Babylonian army at Opis, captured Sippar, and entered Babylon without a battle inside the city. Cyrus himself arrived seventeen days later.
But about a century later, the Greek historian Herodotus told a very different story. In his version, Cyrus diverted the Euphrates into a marsh. The river through the city sank to thigh height. Persian soldiers waded in along the riverbed, right under the walls. And because a festival was underway, the people in the middle of the city didn't even notice.
Xenophon later told a similar tale. But here's the twist: the contemporary Babylonian record never mentions this clever river trick. It says the city was taken without a battle.
So did Babylon fall to a brilliant military trick... or was the real story much simpler?
