How did ancient Rome carry fresh water across hills and valleys, without a single pump?
Rome may have held a million people, and the clean springs were dozens of kilometres away.
So engineers used one force that never rests: gravity.
Surveyors mapped the route with a groma for straight lines, and a long water-level table called a chorobates.
The channel had to slope down the whole way. At Neem, in France, just thirty-four centimetres per kilometre.
Most of it ran underground. Through hills, crews dug shafts and tunnelled between them.
Only where a valley dropped away did they build arches.
Workers cut blocks of up to six tonnes, lifted them with treadwheel cranes, and set them on wooden frames until each arch could hold itself.
Then they sealed the channel with waterproof mortar made from crushed pottery.
When the spring was opened, water flowed for about a day to reach the city: fountains, baths, and homes.
Rome built eleven of these. And one, finished in nineteen B C, still feeds the Trevi Fountain today.
